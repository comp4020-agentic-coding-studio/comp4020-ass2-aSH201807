## 1. Hot Take

- Argues the true source of dread isn't the monster but the realization that "the ordinary explanation would have been safer."
- Worked example: a wet footprint in a long-locked room — showing the clue and letting players choose their response is what makes the moment memorable.

## 2. Horror Starts With Trust

- Before building dread, groups should establish boundaries and tone, discussing:
  - Off-limit themes entirely.
  - Themes allowed only via fade-to-black or indirect reference.
  - Whether body horror, violence, illness, coercion, harm to animals/children, cult activity, or historical discrimination fit the group.
  - Desired intensity, ranging from unsettling mystery to bleak cosmic dread to lurid melodrama.
  - How players can pause or step away without justification.
- Notes historical settings (e.g. the 1920s) require deliberate group decisions about real-world prejudice: "these require consent and a clear purpose, not a claim of period authenticity."

## 3. Build Contrast Before Dread

- Scenes should start ordinary, then introduce one wrong detail — not several at once.
- Table of ordinary element / wrong detail / what it suggests:

| Ordinary element | Wrong detail | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| A family dinner | Every plate is set, but nobody remembers the absent guest | A missing person, altered memory, or deliberate ritual |
| A hospital room | The patient's chart records a treatment that does not exist | A false identity, experimental procedure, or impossible disease |
| A railway platform | The departure board lists a town no map contains | A secret route, an error, or an intrusion into reality |
| A calm harbor | Fish float in a precise circle around one empty boat | A recent event, contamination, or a pattern worth testing |
| A scholar's office | The locked desk is dripping saltwater | A physical impossibility with a traceable source |

- Notes contrast enables intensity variation; light/humorous moments shouldn't be discouraged, since "shared humor can make a table healthier and a later reveal sharper."

## 4. Use Specific, Playable Sensory Detail

- Recommends choosing one or two senses that fit the location.
- Sight examples: a clean line through dust; a lagging reflection; a lit building with no power.
- Sound examples: a knock from a shifting direction; a familiar song at wrong speed; silence where noise is expected.
- Smell examples: brine inland; antiseptic in a forest; burnt paper without a fire.
- Touch examples: condensation inside a coat; warm wallpaper; a vibrating floorboard.
- Taste examples: metal after a phone call; salt in fresh water; smoke with no fire.
- Advises following details with a prompt, e.g.: "The room smells of brine despite being three miles from the harbor. What do you do?"
- Cautions against dictating investigator emotion — prefer physical description ("The brass handle is warm enough to sting your fingers") over stated feelings.

## 5. Make the Wrong Detail Matter

- Recurring signs should connect to causes/consequences via a four-step progression: Signal (group notices inconsistency) → Pattern (inconsistency recurs elsewhere) → Implication (evidence links it to a person/place/deadline) → Choice (investigators decide how to respond).
- Worked example: a dripping sound in a dry apartment is linked to a vanished neighbor's testimony, then linked via a ledger to a warehouse owner; investigators choose whether to search the cellar, watch the owner, or warn the witness before a storm.

## 6. Escalate in Stages

- Table of stage / what the group experiences / what it accomplishes:

| Stage | What the group experiences | What it accomplishes |
|---|---|---|
| Unease | A wrong detail, uneasy witness, missing fact, or contradiction | Creates curiosity without demanding belief |
| Confirmation | A repeated sign, credible testimony, physical trace, or failed mundane explanation | Makes the pattern hard to ignore |
| Encroachment | The threat affects a known person, safe place, or current plan | Makes delay and detachment costly |
| Confrontation | The group encounters the source, its agent, or an immediate consequence | Forces a decision with real stakes |
| Aftermath | The group sees what their choice changed | Gives the scene weight and creates future dread |

- Notes not every scenario needs all stages in order.
- States escalation can be "social, moral, environmental, or personal," not just monster-scale increases.

## 7. Reveal Effects Before Causes

- Suggests showing effects first (impossible injuries, altered rooms, missing time) before causes.
- Recommends revealing only the "portion the group can observe or use," not full lore.
- States a monster's name only matters if it "changes what the group can do."
- Recommends letting mundane explanations remain plausible temporarily so disproving them feels earned.

## 8. Turn Information Into a Choice

- Table of revelation / meaningful choices:

| Revelation | Meaningful choices |
|---|---|
| The witness is being watched | Protect them, use them as bait with consent, move the meeting, or break contact |
| The artifact affects anyone who touches it | Secure it, study it, destroy it, hand it over, or leave it while the threat continues |
| The ritual begins at dawn | Interrupt preparation, seek help, evacuate targets, prepare a countermeasure, or confront the organizer |
| The creature follows a repeated sound | Stop the sound, lure it away, warn others, or use the knowledge to reach a protected location |

- Advises against a single "secretly correct" option — costs should differ, and time/attention itself should be part of the cost.

## 9. Use Silence, Pacing, and Scene Cuts

- Use silence after revelations, not as empty waiting.
- Slow pacing for discoveries and moral choices; speed up routine tasks.
- Cut scenes at a question or consequence rather than exhausting an entire search.
- Use grounded, visible deadlines (a phone, storm, train) rather than arbitrary time-outs: "Randomly announcing that time has run out after a careful plan is not suspense; it is an arbitrary reversal."

## 10. Make Places Carry the Horror

- Recommends locations with four layers: normal purpose, human trace, wrong detail, practical pressure.
- Worked example: an abandoned boarding house with family photos (human trace), unbroken basins of seawater despite broken plumbing (wrong detail), and a returning landlord plus altered sleeper memories (pressure).
- Also suggests using daylight/crowds for fear rather than relying only on darkness.

## 11. Make NPCs Carry Human Fear

- NPC emotional states should have practical effects:
  - Fear → avoidance, hiding records, refusing solitude.
  - Shame → withholding details until trust is earned.
  - Grief → rushing into danger or bad bargains.
  - Obsession → overstating theories, taking evidence, revisiting danger.
  - Denial → protecting institutions, dismissing witnesses, delaying help.
- Warns against treating vulnerable NPCs as "disposable evidence."
- Stresses keeping some allies reliable so players stay emotionally invested.

## 12. Use Handouts, Props, Music, and Light Carefully

- Handouts should be readable and serve the investigation.
- Music kept low enough to hear dialogue.
- Lighting shouldn't obscure sheets, dice, safety tools, or faces.
- Favor a few consistent details over constant stimulation.
- Stop any aid immediately if it distracts or causes discomfort.
- Core clues (dates, names, symbols) must be legible in plain text, not hidden in stylized props.

## 13. Use Sanity as Consequence, Not Decoration

- Sanity checks should mark genuine confrontations with horror, not minor unpleasant details.
- After a result, keep players engaged in the scene rather than sidelined.
- Points to related guides: Sanity System Guide, Temporary Insanity Guide, Bouts of Madness Guide.
- Notes horror can also come from threats to relationships, prior choices, or impossible deadlines — not just Sanity mechanics.

## 14. Avoid Common Horror Failures

- **Hiding all usable information**: mystery needs facts to interpret, not total opacity.
- **Starting at maximum intensity**: opening at full horror works only if there's room to de-escalate; most games benefit from gradual buildup.
- **Repeating the same scare**: repetition (same whispers/shadows) teaches players the pattern; vary the threat's expression.
- **Removing agency to prove danger**: avoid dictating character actions ("you cannot leave"); instead constrain through fiction (a cut bridge, a broken phone line).
- **Making the Keeper the only person who knows what is scary**: watch table reactions and adjust; the goal is "engaged play in the tone the group agreed to create," not silence.

## 15. Horror Scene Checklist

- **Before the scene**: confirm fit with agreed boundaries; establish an ordinary baseline; choose one wrong detail and a matching sense; decide the detail's meaning/direction; give a practical action option.
- **During the scene**: describe facts before requesting rolls/responses; pause after revelations before asking for action; escalate via pattern/consequence/deadline, not random shocks; keep the threat partly unknown but its effects usable; offer choices with different costs, not one correct answer.
- **After the scene**: show what changed from the investigators' decision; allow recovery/regrouping time as needed; carry a detail or consequence forward for continuity; check in or adjust if the scene missed the intended tone.
