# The Convenor's Scenario-Writing Guide

This is a complete guide, in the convenor's own words, covering everything
from picking an inspiration to writing the checks and NPCs that fill out a
finished scenario. It's best suited to a **short** scenario — a single
throughline you can run in one or two sessions — rather than a sprawling
campaign. [Lectures 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10](/lectures/) draw directly on the
sections below.

## 1. Inspiration and genre

Decide what genre the scenario is in before anything else — you can't write
a story without the inspiration for it. Only afterward should you start
pulling in the Mythos' generic elements, checking which of them actually fit
that inspiration, and folding them in to tell a Cthulhu-flavoured story.

There are two broad categories: a straight Call of Cthulhu scenario, or a
fan-fusion scenario that borrows Call of Cthulhu's rules for an entirely
different setting — for example, a *Lord of the Mysteries* fan scenario.

**Finding inspiration.** Look for something you're already interested in —
it only needs to be interesting enough to carry a complete story. That might
be a specific Mythos entity or Great Old One you like, or an original
creature of your own. For example: Ithaqua's range is defined as within the
Arctic Circle, so a scenario built around it could be set in the northwestern
Canadian wilderness in late autumn or winter — the setting of a Derleth story
about a Miskatonic expedition attacked in that region, after which a female
doctor is abducted.

Or find inspiration in your own daily life, then choose Mythos content that
fits it afterward: something you saw in the news, a cold case that's been
open for years, a summer camp that went wrong, a scientific theory or
experiment you got curious about, or a strange trinket you happened to see —
say, a piece of jewellery in a shop window that you can't stop thinking about
after you get home. That's usable.

**Generic Mythos elements**, at the level of the setting itself: ancient,
unknown, chaos, and space.

- *Space* — the Library of Celaeno; a Mythos creature abducted to another
  planet; cyberpunk Cthulhu; or borrowing the tone of something unrelated,
  like *Interstellar*, and treating the Old Ones as having come from outer
  space.
- *Ancient* — many Mythos concepts emphasise "before Earth formed," "before
  humanity existed," "before civilisation was built," foregrounding a sense
  of primordial mystery and dread. Example: as an occultist, you have two
  books in front of you. One is sheepskin parchment, covered in bizarre
  monster drawings you can't make sense of and characters clearly not meant
  for human eyes. The other is thoroughly modern — A4 paper, plain white
  cover, six bold printed characters on the front. Which one draws you in
  more? Usually, the first — the unfamiliar and unexplained reads as more
  mysterious than the familiar.
- *Ancient and unknown together* — e.g. "You're standing on the dock now,
  and in front of you is a group of wrinkled, barely-clothed humanoid
  creatures; what disgusts you is that a fish-like head sits atop each of
  their shoulders. Once they notice you, the creatures open mouths full of
  sharp teeth and charge at you, trailing a foul-smelling yellow slime." Or:
  "A dark shape flits swiftly under the water; an inexplicable smell of
  rotting seaweed; from somewhere behind you, though nothing is visibly
  there, comes a wet, slapping sound of footsteps." A Mythos creature kept
  offstage is often more menacing than one shown in full, and it keeps
  players curious enough to keep investigating.
- Other elements worth drawing on beyond those four: cults (real-world
  examples like Voodoo, the Peoples Temple, Aum Shinrikyo, Heaven's Gate, or
  a distorted variant of a mainstream religion's own doctrine); dreams,
  nightmares, temporal displacement, spatial anomalies; Cthulhu-flavoured
  ancient myths, sacrificial rites, incantations; ancient texts, ruins,
  indigenous villages, archaeological digs; the mentally ill and a mad
  hunger for knowledge; family lineages and dead languages or extinct
  civilisations.

**A worked example.** The "Dulce Base" incident is a well-known urban legend
about an alleged 1979 firefight between Americans and aliens at an
underground facility. From a story like this you can extract concrete,
reusable pieces: the military base itself; underground "Greys"; laser
weapons; a finger vaporised instantly; alien experimentation on humans; and
unnatural underground tunnels.

**Fusing the pieces.** From that Dulce Base backdrop, you could connect to
the K'n-yan from *Trail of Cthulhu* — an underground empire beneath Oklahoma
lit by blue radiance, home to bizarre creatures; long-skulled, brown-skinned
people who fled the surface after a cataclysm. They mainly worship Tulu
(i.e. Cthulhu) and Yig; they once worshipped Tsathoggua too, but abandoned
and destroyed all its shrines after finding something in the dark abyss of
N'kai. K'n-yan communicate telepathically (some gifted individuals can even
create mental illusions) and can dematerialise, existing simultaneously on
the material and astral planes — able to pass through solid matter, or
dematerialise objects they touch. Their weapon is a disintegrator ray-gun
whose damage can't be healed; their protection is an advanced "spirit-cloth"
effective against any attack.

## 2. Planning the scenario's flow

A useful frame for the planning stage: time, place, characters, cause (the
scenario's hook), development (the scenario's body), and result (the
scenario's ending).

**Era.** For example, the 1920s — where weapon and technology choices should
match the decade, so it's worth knowing the period's communication and
transport options and its weapon models — or a modern-day setting instead.

**Duration.** If the plan is a single underground-tunnel expedition, keep it
to 1–3 in-game days.

**Place.** A scenario set in an underground cave or a military base doesn't
need to fuss over which country or city it's in. For example: a secret US
military base near Arkham, Massachusetts, or a newly discovered
archaeological site built around an unnatural underground passage. A
military-base setting limits the roles and backgrounds available to
players — base staff, soldiers, medics. An archaeological-dig setting can
instead be framed as a Miskatonic University expedition — which fits neatly,
since Miskatonic's active period in the setting is mostly the 1920s–30s.

**Hooks.** Players can be pulled into the scenario passively — everyone just
rented a cabin in the woods for a memorable Christmas, and only after
arriving do they discover the forest has a Wendigo in it; or they're simply
eating at a fast-food place when they're suddenly saddled with guardianship
of an orphan; or someone wakes up to find they've lost their memory. Or
players can choose to get involved themselves — they receive an anonymous
letter out of nowhere, or a friend or family member goes missing, or they
receive a death threat; or the police, stuck on a difficult case, ask
civilians for help, and the PCs decide to form a team.

Worked example: Miskatonic's archaeology team loses contact shortly after
entering an underground cave, and three days pass with no one emerging, so
the university organises a search-and-rescue team to investigate. The
missing-persons angle creates more tension than a plain archaeological
expedition — and the human-experimentation angle noted above is good
material to fold in as well.

**Structure: open vs. linear.**

- *Open* — many branches, many scenes, rich in clues, high freedom. This
  demands more of the writer.
- *Linear* — compact construction, tight plot, good for a short session. You
  only need one throughline to string the scenes together, which keeps what
  the design needs simple: fix the key scenes players will pass through, and
  make sure each one carries the key clue that pulls them into the next.
  Decide in advance how players will obtain each clue (see clue design and
  checks, below), then string the scenes together into a complete story.

Worked example (linear): players take a lift down into an underground
tunnel → explore the tunnel → discover the K'n-yan research base, or come
under attack → the players are killed, used as test subjects, or escape with
their lives. The core scenes here are only the tunnel and the K'n-yan base —
but two scenes is too short to carry enough clues, so add scenes purely to
deliver clues: a pre-tunnel investigation phase, talking with the people
organising the rescue effort to get background from the university, and
exploring the archaeology team's surface camp — which can be extended into a
nearby indigenous village near the tunnel, giving both social and
literature-type clues. Aside from the K'n-yan (who can't be talked to), this
gives players chances to gain clues through conversation, so a player who
put points into social skills actually gets to use them.

Design principle: remember the scenario is for players, so when designing
scenes, clues, or events, always account for what players might expect —
some players enjoy combat more, others enjoy gathering clues and reasoning
out the truth.

Refined flow: explore the surface camp / talk to the rescuers → enter and
explore the tunnel → come under attack / enter the base → ending. The
tunnel-to-base transition is where you lean on the Mythos creature: use the
K'n-yan to build atmosphere, which can also drain Sanity or set up the
reveal and climax.

Worked example (the tunnel scene): the tunnel itself is monotonous, so clues
are hard to place and exploration space is limited — but this is where the
K'n-yan's telepathy comes in. Have the K'n-yan notice the players shortly
after they enter the tunnel, and use guided mental illusions to lure them in
as test subjects. This enriches the tunnel section and builds atmosphere,
since illusions are usually something that only happens to someone on the
edge of losing their mind. Example: when the players try to backtrack, they
find the spot where the lift used to be is simply gone. This pressures
players' Sanity while also stopping them from feeling like they can just
give up halfway. As long as it doesn't break the game's rules, an existing
setting can be extended and adapted quite a bit.

## 3. Clue design

Clues split into two broad categories: **guiding clues**, which give player
characters direction for where to explore and act next, and **revealing
clues**, which cover everything else — the solution to a puzzle, information
about the scenario's Mythos content — mainly used to push the plot forward
and explain it, while also working as a small reward for a player character
who actively investigates.

**On guiding clues.** A session is really a chain of scenes strung together,
and it's the guiding clues that link those scenes and locations. Neither the
Keeper nor the players want a PC stuck in one scene not knowing what to do —
it wastes time and saps everyone's motivation to keep going. Even a player
with big ideas who can decide their own next investigative target can't be
relied on to always pick the location that happens to trigger the intended
plot; for a new Keeper especially, players wandering into an unplanned scene
is close to guaranteed to stall the session, or force the Keeper to hand
over a core clue early. These clues don't take much deliberate thought —
anything connected to your next intended scene, placed so it doesn't feel
out of place in the current one, works. For example: if you want the PCs to
head to the library next, put a library card, or a book recently borrowed
from that library, in the current scene, or have an NPC mention in
conversation that "the missing person visited the library the day they
disappeared."

There's also an implicit, semi-metagame convention in CoC: anything the
Keeper specifically calls out in a description is assumed by the players to
matter. For example: "In the victim's suitcase you find a change of
clothes, toiletries, and an oddly shaped little statue." Any player, however
low their character's intelligence, will go look at that statue — because
in real life, you wouldn't miss it either.

Guiding clues can be explicit or implicit, and you can offer more than one
direction. Treating "no guiding clues" as a test of the players' deduction,
or taking pride in stalling them, isn't good design — unless your players
tell you directly they don't want guiding clues, their enjoyment comes
first.

**On revealing clues.** Harder to generalise, since they depend entirely on
the specific story — but for a scenario investigating a murder committed by
a person or Mythos creature, the clues roughly split into three groups:

1. *Clues around the victim's body* — time of death, extent of damage,
   external examination findings (wounds), central-nervous-system damage
   (brain and spinal cord), internal examination findings, whether poisoning
   is present, and so on. Even if you don't want PCs performing the autopsy
   themselves, provide at least a reasonably complete autopsy report — the
   manner of death is itself information about the killer or creature: where
   the attacks were aimed, its typical methods, and, in a serial case, even
   what it seems to select victims for.
2. *Clues around the crime scene* — physical evidence such as fingerprints,
   footprints, claw marks, bullets, documents, and burned material, or
   biological evidence such as blood, saliva, hair, plant pollen, or a deep
   one's scales. Guiding clues often live here too, but the crime scene is
   one of the best places to lay out detail and hint at the killer or
   creature. A scenario aiming for a strong Mythos atmosphere should use
   every means available to convey a creature's strangeness and horror; even
   a scenario aiming for a "perfect murder" with no Mythos element at all
   should still make its misdirection something players have to think
   through, rather than "the killer was clever and cleaned up all the
   evidence, so the PCs find nothing" — the scenario still has to be
   playable.
3. *Clues about the plot itself* — running a session is, at bottom, a way of
   telling a story, and a story without a plot is unbearable to sit through.
   Whatever genre or style you're writing, you should already know what
   story you want to tell, and your clues exist to "explain" that story to
   the players in place of your own voice — a Keeper can't spell everything
   out through narration without the session losing its point. If the story
   centres on a Mythos creature, pass along creature-related information
   through old records or a nursery rhyme passed down by word of mouth; if
   it's a grudge-and-vengeance story, pass the plot along through
   tear-stained diary pages, or words scratched into a wall.

**Delivery formats.** Literature-type clues (old books, specific documents,
diaries, notes), social-type clues (obtained through talking with an NPC),
and trace-type clues (physical evidence). Splitting clues across these
formats matters mainly because if PCs spend the whole session buried in the
library reading one book after another, that gets strange fast — and too
much repeated content is a flaw in both scenarios and novels.

- *Literature clues*: because the information comes straight from a book or
  document — and modern-day sessions also have imba tools like phones and
  computers — getting this kind of clue is close to guaranteed, barring
  truly bad luck on a Library Use roll. Put your broadest, most foundational
  clues in this format.
- *Social clues*: obtained through NPC conversation. When your scenario has
  clear structure and clear relationships between people, you can tie a
  clue exclusively to one specific NPC — but if a PC misses that NPC for
  whatever reason and the Keeper isn't the improvising type, the result is a
  broken clue chain at best and a stalled session at worst. The better
  approach is to list out everything obtainable through NPC conversation as
  a set, and hand pieces of it out based on how the actual conversation with
  PCs goes — this doesn't weaken an NPC's distinctiveness, since what makes
  an NPC memorable is how the Keeper plays them, not "only they can tell you
  X." Background information tied to daily life fits well here, and
  depending on who the PCs are talking to (occultists, Miskatonic
  professors, and so on) it can carry Mythos content too.
- *Trace clues*: the most fragmentary and scattered kind. When your scenario
  has a Mythos creature in it, lean on trace clues to reinforce the
  creature's presence, and save a direct confrontation for when you need to
  speed up the pacing or the plot is already near its boiling point.
  Otherwise you risk two failure modes: "crying wolf" — sending a lone
  creature to harass the PCs every few scenes, which might read as tense the
  first couple of times but becomes a joke by the third, and frequent
  repeated combat rounds tend to interrupt the flow of exploration and
  deduction — or an overpowered creature ending the session early, since
  investigators' combat capability is normally kept tightly controlled and
  recovers much more slowly than in combat-focused systems, while many
  Mythos creatures have overwhelming combat advantages. A total party kill
  or bad end isn't itself something to avoid — going mad together is the
  Cthulhu default, and a happy ending is the surprising outcome — but as a
  player, falling into madness while chasing the truth (or what you believe
  is the truth) is far more satisfying than being drained by a hound that
  shows up for no clear reason while you understand nothing. Players'
  enjoyment comes first.

## 4. Puzzle design

Puzzles broadly fall into a few types: object-hunting puzzles, pattern
puzzles, wordplay puzzles, and decoding puzzles. None of these types ranks
above another — which one fits depends on the clue's content.

**Difficulty isn't the same as quality.** A "hard" puzzle in a solving game
isn't automatically a "good" one. A puzzle exists to be solved, and a
scenario isn't ranked by its difficulty — only by whether the players got
enjoyment from it. If you know your players well, set puzzles that match
their taste and still take some real thought to crack, which both drives
their motivation to solve and gives them a sense of achievement once they
do. For a one-off group you don't know well, either mix several puzzle
types into the scenario and pick on the fly based on how players react, or
be flexible enough to build an appropriately-difficult puzzle on the spot —
though new Keepers or scenario authors attempting this without much
practice risk hurting the experience, so an ad-hoc one-shot is often safer
run with an already-tested scenario.

**Purpose first.** Be clear about why you're setting a puzzle at all. If
it's purely to hand over a clue, why not just have the PC roll a Spot Hidden
or Library Use check instead? For some situations, a skill check genuinely
is the better call — it's one of the distinct pleasures CoC can offer. But
when you deliberately set a puzzle for a specific clue, you should have
something you want to communicate to the players, and the puzzle and its
solving process is exactly how you pass that idea along.

Worked example: (a) investigating a missing-person case, PCs use Spot
Hidden and find a badge with a strange design in the desk drawer; (b)
investigating the same case, PCs use Spot Hidden to notice a bookshelf
against the wall looks like it can be pushed, and behind it is a hidden
compartment holding a small four-digit combination safe, which the PCs open
using a reference chart found under the bed, revealing the same badge with
the strange design. Physically, is there any difference in outcome? None —
both times a badge is found, and the second version might even burn ten or
twenty extra minutes on lengthy thinking and description. But is that
actually wasted time? Add one assumption: the badge is a cult member's ID
tag, and by examining its design PCs can trace the missing person's
connection to that cult, giving them their next investigative direction —
in other words, this is a guiding clue. Given that, the ten or twenty
minutes is completely worth it: a badge tossed carelessly in a drawer might
not draw attention, but a badge locked away behind layers of security
absolutely cannot be ignored. That's the message this puzzle — not a hard
one to solve — naturally communicates: this badge matters a great deal to
the missing person, and is very likely a key clue to finding them. Mixed in
instead with other information obtained through plain skill checks, PCs may
well overlook its guiding role and stall out — especially likely in a
linear scenario — and the cost then is far more than the ten or twenty
minutes.

So: a puzzle is worth setting for clues that are clearly guiding and
important to the plot — but given the time cost and the scenario's length,
keep puzzles limited to those key clues; everything else can be obtained
through the faster route of a skill check.

**Design points:**

1. A clear goal and a clear solution. This is the core of every puzzle —
   but for the scenario author, what matters more is being clear yourself
   about the solution, since PCs often won't know in advance which clue a
   given puzzle points to (otherwise why bother solving it?) — see the
   badge example above.
2. Gradual difficulty. You wouldn't hand a child who's still learning their
   ABCs an entire book in a language they can't read and expect them to get
   through it. Make sure your puzzle guides players step by step from a
   shallow starting point toward the solution, especially for players used
   to just looking the answer up online.
3. Don't invert means and ends. Don't design a puzzle purely for the sake of
   designing a puzzle — always remember a puzzle exists to serve the plot,
   not the other way around.
4. Running puzzles in parallel often pays off. Give PCs two or more puzzles
   related to the same answer at once: when they're stuck on one, they can
   work on the other, then use its result to work backward into the one
   they couldn't crack. This asks more of the scenario author, but tends to
   land well at the table — and for a written scenario it isn't hard to
   pull off: place multiple guiding clues in the same scene, and PCs get
   more varied options for where to investigate next. Look closely and
   you'll notice this is, in effect, exactly what an open-structure
   scenario already is — an open scenario isn't some especially advanced
   technique, it just asks a bit more of the Keeper's ability to track the
   timeline and adapt on the fly.

## 5. Check and Sanity design

**Don't let a check become the point where the session gets stuck.** In a
multi-branch scenario you can use checks to gate how many clues PCs pick
up, but in a simpler linear scenario, if a PC misses a key clue — or the
clue pointing to the next scene — because a check failed, the Keeper is left
either improvising a new thread outside the existing structure, or letting
everyone walk toward a total party kill. For a linear scenario, think
carefully about whether to gate key or guiding clues behind a check at all;
setting a moderately difficult puzzle instead, for interest and
controllability, is one reasonable option.

**A check still needs narration, not just a pass/fail readout.** Example:
Keeper: "You open the cabinet to see what's inside — roll a Spot Hidden."
Player A rolls and fails. Keeper: "You don't notice anything worth
mentioning." Player B rolls and succeeds. Keeper: "You notice a revolver
sitting in plain view in the cabinet." Wait — a revolver isn't worth
mentioning? This is also why players joke "guess I'm blind" or "guess I'm
deaf" after a failed Spot Hidden or Listen roll — and, mechanically, that's
actually correct; the only problem is in the wording. The fix is simple:
change the success description to something like "you notice a hidden
compartment in the bottom of the cabinet — one you happen to recognise from
a book or film you've seen — and inside it is a revolver." That explains why
the other PC missed it, while still giving the successful roll a sense of
achievement. This is something you can avoid entirely at the writing stage,
just by checking the rulebook's description of the relevant skill before
setting a check — each skill's entry already spells out, with examples,
what kind of thing that skill is meant to reveal (in this case, that Spot
Hidden should turn up things that are "not obvious"), and your
success/failure text should reflect that.

**Match how many checks you call for to your specific players' taste.**
Different people want different things from a session — the satisfaction of
a successful deduction, the thrill of a good fight, the tension of a
hopeless situation — and checks are no different: some players love the
uncertainty and surprise of the dice, including a spectacular failure;
others can't stand how uncontrollable dice are. Take that into account: for
players who dislike checks, swap a clue's delivery for a puzzle or a
wordplay game instead; for players who dislike combat rounds, swap the
scenario's intended dramatic peak from a fight to a negotiation with the
mastermind, or an unwinnable arrival of an Old One instead.

**On Sanity checks.** Madness is the *result* of a Sanity check, not its
purpose. Tearing up a character sheet doesn't mean "you won" or "the player
just wasn't as clever as you" — a Sanity check should only reflect the
possible bad outcome of an action, or a situation the rulebook defines as
one that may cause a Sanity check, nothing more. (If your players
explicitly ask for more Sanity checks because they enjoy them, all of the
above is void — give them a Sanity feast.)

When a Sanity check might cause temporary or indefinite insanity, consider
dialling down the difficulty of the fight or negotiation that follows. Treat
a PC with a strong chance of falling into temporary insanity as effectively
absent for a while — at least for the immediately following combat or
search — and set the following checks or combat difficulty a little lower
than you would with everyone at full strength.

## 6. NPC design

**Be clear about the NPC's purpose.** Before placing an NPC in your
scenario, decide what role they're actually meant to play — guiding PCs to
the next scene, delivering a hidden clue, or acting as a pawn sent by the
mastermind to mislead them — and write that down explicitly. This matters
because it preserves your (or another Keeper's) control over the scenario:
a session's actual events are hard to predict, so an NPC without a clear
guiding purpose is exactly what leaves an improvisation-averse Keeper stuck,
or pushes them into handing out clues too early.

Also worth noting: "lots of NPCs" doesn't mean "rich content," especially in
a linear scenario. If your scenario is investigating a murder where the
killer is an outsider who happened to pass the victim's building and struck
on impulse, you could give every resident in that building a full name and
a few paragraphs of backstory — but how much of that would actually be
useful? Dense, richly detailed scenarios are fine, but whatever you're
writing, the proportion of your text that's actually load-bearing determines
the quality of the work — so it's worth starting from the parts that look
simple but matter, rather than padding.

**Give the NPC a distinctive trait.** This might be an old point, but it's
still the real measure of whether an NPC's characterisation landed. You
might forget most of a scenario's PC names, but remember an NPC who "looks
down on people with low IQ," or forget every PC's nickname but remember the
one NPC who mercilessly shot down the group's signal without any mercy.
That's what a successful NPC looks like. In practice, even an experienced
Keeper's NPCs can start blurring together due to mood or ingrained social
habits — that improves with practice and self-awareness — but it shouldn't
stop you from giving NPCs clearly distinct traits at the writing stage.

In play, that trait usually shows through appearance or personality, and
dialogue should fit the NPC's identity and era: it's hard to believe a
noblewoman rhapsodising about slum life, or a lifelong deep-sea fisherman
who can rattle off the finer points of livestock postnatal care — that's a
mismatch of identity. If you've set the scenario in the 1920s, a physicist
obviously can't pull out a photo of a black hole to show you (unless a
hound has already taken an interest in him) — that's a mismatch of era.
These details might seem minor, but they're part of what makes a scenario
feel a little more convincing.

**Avoid long NPC-to-NPC dialogue.** Running a session is a story you build
together with your players — not a performance you put on for yourself
while they watch. If you genuinely can't avoid having two NPCs negotiate at
length with the PCs only listening in, summarise the gist of that exchange
for your players instead of reading it out line by line.
