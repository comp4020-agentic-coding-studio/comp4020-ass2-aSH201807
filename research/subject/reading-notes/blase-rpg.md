**Abstract/summary** (the page has an introductory paragraph rather than a
formal abstract — this is my one-paragraph summary of the whole piece): A
Call of Cthulhu scenario isn't a trail of rooms investigators must visit in
order; good design gives the Keeper a stable hidden truth while giving
players several different ways to reach it. The article lays out a 16-part
framework, starting from "choose a strong question" through to "prepare
endings, not an ending," and demonstrates most of it using a single running
example — an heir trying to recover a family artifact hidden in a coastal
warehouse.

## 1. Hot Take
"The climax is the least useful place to start designing a Call of Cthulhu
scenario." Argues design should start from the underlying situation, not the
final confrontation.

## 2. Start With a Situation, Not a Plot
"A plot tells you what happens in order. A situation tells you what is true,
who is acting..." **The running example first appears here** (full
reconstruction below).

## 3. Choose a Strong Scenario Question
Good-question examples given:
- "Why did a reliable person vanish after receiving a particular letter?"
- "Who is staging the accidents around a construction site, and why?"
- "What is making a family ill after they move into an inherited house?"
- "Why are several unrelated witnesses describing the same impossible sound?"
- "What did a dead collector remove from the museum before their death?"

Contrast pair: "Which Outer God is behind this?" (bad — too Mythos-forward,
too fast) vs. "Why did the astronomer destroy their own telescope?" (better
— concrete, grounded starting point).

## 4. Write the Hidden Truth
"The hidden truth is the Keeper's answer key." Six-question framework: what
happened before play / what does the antagonist want / what are they doing
now / what do they fear or need to hide / what makes the threat unnatural or
horrific / what can change the outcome. Notably distinguishes belief from
reality — e.g. a cultist may misunderstand the ritual they're performing.

## 5. Build Layers of Mystery
"Call of Cthulhu scenarios work well when each answer creates a deeper
question." Four layers: surface event → human cause → hidden mechanism →
response. A layer "can be emotional or practical," not just lore-based.

## 6. Design a Redundant Clue Web
"The core design rule is simple: every conclusion the investigators need
should have [multiple evidence paths]." **Subsection — Core, supporting, and
advantage clues**: core clues "keep the case moving and should be reachable
through ordinary investigation" (never gated behind one failed roll);
supporting clues confirm theories/add context; advantage clues make the
final plan safer/faster/more effective without being mandatory. The running
example's clue-web table appears here (see case reconstruction below).

## 7. Give the Antagonist an Active Timeline
"The antagonist should act while investigators investigate." Three-to-five
escalating stages tied to visible signals (locked office/frightened contact
→ increased guards/disappearances → strange weather/ritual signs →
confrontation/pursuit/rescue). Clock advances on story time passing,
investigator actions creating visibility, failed rolls, or deliberate
antagonist choices — never just because players paused to think.

## 8. Create Locations That Change Play — five-part template
1. **Immediate impression** — what an investigator notices on arrival
2. **Question** — what can be learned or accomplished there
3. **Evidence** — clues, objects, or people present
4. **Pressure** — what makes staying or returning costly
5. **Connection** — where the evidence leads next

Worked example (independent of the running artifact/heir case): an abandoned
greenhouse that looks tended despite broken glass. Investigators learn who's
harvesting a rare plant; evidence includes delivery labels, tracks, a
notebook, an exhausted caretaker; pressure comes from fumes, an approaching
owner, and plants reacting after dark; connection is a supplier's address
and a name in the notebook.

## 9. Design NPCs as Pressure Points — goal / fear / resource / threshold
"An NPC exists to change the group's options." Table example using the
running case's three NPCs (dock foreman, retired sailor, heir) — see case
reconstruction below.

## 10. Make the Opening Impossible to Ignore
Contrast example: a bare anonymous letter (weak, ignorable) vs. one that
"quotes a private detail," arrives with a damaged personal object, and warns
a named person will disappear at sunset (strong, playable).

## 11. Use Handouts as Investigative Tools
"A handout should do work the spoken description cannot." Example handout
types:
- A damaged letter with a missing line and return address
- A map marked with three locations and an abruptly stopped route
- A photograph showing an object later found elsewhere
- A receipt, ledger page, or train ticket contradicting an alibi
- A short newspaper clipping establishing a past event with an unexplained
  detail

## 12. Choose a Scenario Structure
- **Linear investigation** — "a clear beginning, middle, and end"; good for
  new Keepers, conventions, fixed-time sessions. No specific worked example.
- **Node-based investigation** — offers several leads from the start;
  recommends four to six nodes with multiple connections. Example given:
  "The warehouse, archive, relative, and shipping office can be visited in
  any order" — a generic illustrative list, **not** a further narrative
  extension of the heir/artifact story (see note in case reconstruction).
- **Sandbox/campaign situation** — establishes factions, resources, and
  timelines for ongoing play. No specific worked example.

## 13. Plan Action and Horror for a Purpose
"Action and horror should reveal something, change access, raise the cost of
delay..." Generic illustrative scenes (not tied to the running example):
- A chase that shows where a cultist goes, forces a choice between pursuit
  and helping an injured ally, or leaves behind dropped evidence
- A fight that buys time to protect a witness, or reveals mundane weapons
  won't work

## 14. Prepare Endings, Not an Ending
"Write several possible outcomes based on what investigators might
accomplish." Table of outcomes tied to the running example — see case
reconstruction below.

## 15. Build a Scenario From Scratch — 10-step workflow
"Use this sequence when you need a playable original scenario quickly."
Steps: choose an ordinary starting world → introduce one wrong thing → write
the hidden truth → write the opening, linking the anomaly to investigators
with one lead → name four nodes pursuable in any order → create three clues
per necessary conclusion across varied routes → write the antagonist
timeline (three escalating moves, each visible) → prepare two useful NPCs
and one dangerous NPC (each with goal/fear/resource/threshold) → choose one
action scene and one horror revelation, each with investigative purpose →
list possible endings, including at least one success that doesn't depend on
killing anything. Noted as fitting "on two pages of notes."

## 16. Scenario Design Checklist (verbatim items)
- A one-paragraph situation explaining the past event, current threat, and
  opening hook
- A central question the investigators can understand without Mythos
  knowledge
- A short hidden truth that ends when play begins
- Several layers of discovery, each creating a practical new question
- At least three evidence paths to every necessary conclusion
- Core clues that remain available after ordinary failure
- An antagonist timeline with visible, logical escalation
- Locations that each offer evidence, pressure, and a connection onward
- NPCs with goals, fears, resources, and limits
- Handouts that create questions rather than decorative reading
- An opening that supplies both connection and motion
- Action and horror scenes that change the investigation
- Several possible endings and consequences, not a scripted conclusion

## Detailed case reconstruction: the shipping-family / warehouse artifact case

The article's one running example, pieced together from every place it
appears, in story order.

**Setup** (from §2, verbatim): *"Because a shipping family concealed an
artifact in a coastal warehouse decades ago, the heir of a failed
antiquarian now wants to recover it before the building is demolished."*

**Antagonist's current actions** (§2): *"They are bribing dockworkers,
falsifying cargo records, and silencing a former partner."*

**Countdown / deadline** (§2): *"If nobody intervenes, they will open the
artifact during the next spring tide"* — the case's implicit clock, echoing
the "antagonist timeline" principle from §7.

**Investigator hook / opening** (§2): *"the investigators become involved
when the former partner arrives at a hospital carrying a water-damaged
ledger and dies before explaining why they sought them out."* Matches the
§10 principle that a strong opening needs a private detail plus urgency,
not just an anonymous letter.

**Redundant clue web** (§6 table) — three independent paths to each of
three conclusions:
- *"The heir is involved"*: a dockworker saw them meeting the foreman; their
  name appears in a forged cargo record; someone searches the investigators'
  rooms after they ask about the warehouse.
- *"The warehouse is dangerous at high tide"*: a retired sailor refuses to
  go there after dark; a tide chart is marked in the dead partner's ledger;
  seawater rises from a locked floor drain at the wrong hour.
- *"The artifact must not be opened"*: a former associate begs the group to
  destroy it; a journal describes an earlier disaster; the heir grows
  desperate when investigators threaten to move it.

**Key NPCs** (§9 table):
- **Dock foreman** — goal: keep the job and avoid scandal; fear: the heir
  will expose their theft; resources: keys, schedules, worker names;
  threshold: proof the heir plans to abandon them. Gives access but expects
  silence.
- **Retired sailor** — goal: protect local families; fear: being called
  crazy again; resources: tide knowledge, old maps, a warning; threshold:
  respect and evidence the danger is real. Helps only if the group keeps a
  promise.
- **Heir** — goal: recover the artifact; fear: losing status and control;
  resources: money, legal access, hired help; threshold: a threat to the
  artifact or their public reputation.

**Structure note**: in §12's node-based example, "the warehouse, archive,
relative, and shipping office can be visited in any order" is listed — but
this is a generic illustrative list, not a further narrative extension of
this specific story (the archive/relative/shipping-office are never tied
back into the heir/artifact plot elsewhere in the article).

**Possible endings** (§14 table):
- **Containment**: seal the warehouse and deny access at high tide — cost:
  the artifact remains and someone must watch it.
- **Destruction**: break the object using a dangerous method — cost:
  evidence is lost and the group draws unwanted attention.
- **Exposure**: connect the heir to the crimes publicly — the heir may
  escape, but their network collapses.
- **Bargain**: trade the artifact for a victim's life — the group saves
  someone while creating a future debt.
- **Failure with survival**: flee the harbor and warn others — the threat
  spreads, but the campaign gains a new urgent objective.

This case runs through §2, 6, 9, 12 (partially), and 14 — the article's only
sustained example. §8 (locations) uses an unrelated greenhouse example, and
§13 (action/horror) and §3 (question-choosing) use only generic,
unconnected illustrative lines.
