## 1. Hot Take

- Argues the key GM skill is choosing answers that create meaningful next decisions, not hoarding secret knowledge.
- States clues should redirect the story.
- Quoted line: "Information is not the goal. Decisions are the goal."

## 2. The Keeper's First Rule

- The GM should act as a fair, honest moderator rather than an adversary.
- Keep fictional secrets, but stay transparent about risks and consequences.
- Notes CoC doesn't guarantee character survival, which is why transparency about risk matters.

## 3. Before Anyone Rolls

- Prep should be minimal: a scenario truth, opening pressure, clue paths, active NPCs, and a sense of stakes if players stall.
- A prep checklist table lists: tone/boundaries, premise, scenario truth, starting scene, clue network, NPCs, rules reference.
- Also covers setting content boundaries and keeping the table functional (dice, handouts, lighting).

## 4. Give Players a Usable Premise

- Four elements make a workable premise: setting/time, the inciting problem, investigator connection, expected action types.
- Worked example: a 1927 New York museum-assistant disappearance premise.
- Advises letting players link personal backstory to the hook.
- Advises using pregenerated investigators for one-shots with visible key info.

## 5. Open the First Scene With Pressure

- Open with an active disruption, not passive scene-setting.
- The opening should clarify: what's wrong, why investigators are involved, what they can do first.
- Recommends starting mid-scene (e.g. already at the crime scene) rather than before it.

## 6. Frame Every Scene Around a Question

- Every scene needs a driving question; otherwise summarize and skip ahead.
- Sample scene questions given: witness testimony, letter sender, archive access, etc.
- End scenes once the question resolves or the pressure shifts.

## 7. Describe First, Roll Second

- State ordinary facts before requestable rolls, then ask player intent.
- Table mapping four situations to Keeper responses:

| Situation | Keeper response |
|---|---|
| Answer obvious/success assured | Give info, no roll |
| Action can't work | Explain and redirect |
| Failure changes nothing | Give result/summarize |
| Success/failure both matter | Name skill/difficulty, then roll |

## 8. Choose the Right 7e Procedure

- **Standard skill rolls**: state skill/difficulty before rolling; use success levels for extra benefits.
- **Pushed rolls**: require a stated escalation/cost, not a free retry; failure should reflect that risk. Links to the Pushing Rolls Guide (`/articles/coc-7e/skills/push-the-roll`).
- **Idea rolls and stalled play**: use to help players connect known clues, not to invent new ones.
- **Sanity checks**: reserve for genuinely shocking events; links to Sanity System Guide (`/articles/coc-7e/sanity-system`) and Sanity Checks Guide (`/articles/coc-7e/sanity-system/sanity-checks`).

## 9. Keep Clues Moving

- Recommends multiple paths to each required conclusion via three clue categories:

| Category | Purpose | Delivery |
|---|---|---|
| Core clue | Advances required plot | Obvious evidence, witness, record |
| Supporting clue | Confirms theory/fills gaps | Skill roll, secondary source, handout |
| Advantage clue | Gives leverage/edge | Hidden detail, strong success, prep |

- Also covers handling red herrings honestly.
- Recommends using handouts with actionable inconsistencies.

## 10. Run Conversations as Investigation

- NPCs need known goals, wants, fears, and limits.
- Not every talk needs a roll — reserve rolls for real obstacles.
- Skill distinctions given: "Charm creates goodwill and makes a relationship easier."
- "Fast Talk buys a short opening, distraction, or plausible excuse."
- "Intimidate applies pressure and may create a future enemy."
- Persuade takes time via reasoned argument.
- Psychology interprets behavior only.

## 11. Manage Split Groups and Spotlight Time

- Splitting the party is fine if scenes cut quickly between groups.
- Suggests direct prompts to quiet players.
- Confirms players control only their own characters' actions.

## 12. Use Pacing to Protect Attention

- Pacing varies by scene type: slow down for big moments, speed through logistics.
- Lists pressure-escalation examples that should always stem from established fiction: missed deadlines, unavailable contacts, weather changes, contradicting clues.

## 13. Handle Combat, Chases, and Sudden Danger

- Combat/chases signal a shift in pressure, not the investigation's end.
- Use full procedures only when tactical decisions matter.
- Links: Combat and Chases Guide (`/articles/coc-7e/combat-chases`); When to Fight, When to Flee Guide (`/articles/coc-7e/combat-chases/when-to-fight-when-to-flee`).

## 14. Improvise Without Losing the Truth

- Four questions guide improvisation: what's true given scenario facts; which NPC/clue could connect; what's risked; what changes after.
- Advises moving clue *delivery*, not meaning.
- Advises honoring clever unexpected solutions.

## 15. Close the Session Cleanly

- End sessions at natural narrative beats, not exhausted notes.
- For one-shots: protect time for the climax.
- For campaigns: end with a clear forward hook and record what was learned, what changed/was damaged, who now knows the party, promises/losses, and next pressure.
- Suggests brief post-session feedback.

## 16. At-the-Table Checklist

- Broken into Start, Per-Scene, Stall-Recovery, and End phases.
- Covers premise statement, boundary reminders, handout placement, roll discipline, clue delivery, and end-of-session recordkeeping.
