- **Title**: Learning from Ludemes: An Inventory of Common Player Actions

- **Authors**: Jeremy Riel (University of Illinois Chicago) & Rob Monahan
  (North Carolina State University)
- **Date**: 2024
- **Type**: peer-reviewed academic journal article (qualitative research)
- **URL**: https://journals.uu.se/IJRP/article/view/325 (DOI

**Citation**: Riel, J., & Monahan, R. (2024). Learning from Ludemes: An
Inventory of Common Player Actions within Tabletop Role-Playing Games
(TTRPGs) to Inform Principled Design of Game-Based Learning Experiences.
*International Journal of Role-Playing*, (15), 178–210.
https://doi.org/10.33063/ijrp.vi15.325

**Abstract** (p.178, verbatim): In this study, the authors investigate
role-playing game design through the lens of instructional design practice
to probe the ecosystem of tabletop role-playing games (TTRPGs) and their
applied use for learning. A qualitative thematic analysis approach is used
to systematically catalog and categorize all possible player actions,
called ludemes, from the 5th Edition of Dungeons & Dragons (D&D). Through
this analysis, the interplay of game mechanics, player actions, and rules
of TTRPGs are explored, unveiling their potential to stimulate various
psychological, environmental, and behavioral factors that positively
influence learning processes. Specifically, this study's analysis of a
game's ludemes (i.e., player actions) exposes the interrelationships,
congruence, and combinations of actions with various learning processes.
The identified categories of ludemes in the study pave the way toward a
working vocabulary and framework for future research in the application of
game-based learning and its potential to catalyze meaningful learning
outcomes. From a global instructional standpoint, this study highlights the
role of pedagogical affordances, such as collaborative storytelling and
problem solving skills, theorizing how TTRPGs can promote cognitive,
metacognitive, affective, social, and cultural learning. Toward these
goals, the authors posit that role-playing, with its complex dynamics and
compelling narratives, offers a robust and natural conduit for learning.

## 1. INTRODUCTION (pp.178–180)

- p.178: active learning outperforms rote memorization but is hard to bring
  into formal education; game-based learning (GBL) as a promising strategy;
  TTRPGs defined as collaborative participatory narratives guided by a game
  master (GM).
- p.179: Kolb's (1984) four-stage experiential learning cycle; RPGs as a
  "safe space" for identity/strategy experimentation (Gee 2003); situated
  cognition (Lave & Wenger 1991); expert-level modeling examples — players
  strategizing like a military commander, negotiating like a diplomat,
  solving puzzles like a detective.
- pp.179–180: core gap identified — no comprehensive inventory of player
  actions and their expected learning outcomes exists; the paper's method
  (thematic analysis of D&D 5e) is introduced; 37 ludeme categories
  identified.

## 2. BACKGROUND (pp.180–187)

**2.1 Real Learning by Playing Pretend** (pp.180–181): RPGs used across
nursing/surgery/dentistry/international relations/history education
(p.180); the "triple-layered interaction" phenomenon — player strategy
layer, in-character layer, and a meta-level experiencing layer (p.181);
player actions vs. character actions distinction (p.181).

**2.2 Understanding Learning through Gameplay with the Social Cognitive
Theory Lens** (pp.181–183): Bandura's (1986) triadic reciprocal determinism
model, introduced p.181, depicted in Figure 1 (p.182); self-regulation —
self-monitoring, self-evaluation, self-reinforcement (p.182); observational
learning and the role of social/cultural factors (p.182); participation as
the operative mechanism of learning within SCT (pp.182–183).

**2.3 SDT and Motivational Aspects of Gameplay** (pp.183–184):
Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan 1985) — competence, autonomy,
relatedness (p.183); intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation in TTRPGs (p.183);
demotivation — what happens when the three intrinsic needs are blocked
(p.183).

**2.4 Prioritizing Player Actions: The Ludeme as a Unit of Participation in
Games** (pp.184–185): game mechanics vs. player actions distinction, citing
Mariais et al. (2010) and Montola (2008) (p.184); ludeme defined as the
basic unit of play a player can perform (Parlett n.d.) — propagates between
games like a meme (p.184); reusable ludeme examples — moving, capturing,
reaching the finish, attacking, defending, upgrading, collaborating
(pp.184–185).

**2.5 Without a Table to Roll On** (pp.185–187): lack of a consistently
held ludeme taxonomy in TTRPG research (p.185); reasons for choosing D&D
5e — historically the first mass-played TTRPG, most popular by sales,
influential on the genre (p.186); explicit scope limitation — only
player-character (PC) rules analyzed, Dungeon Master (DM) role excluded
because it's covered mainly in a different rulebook (pp.186–187); necessary
vs. sufficient ludemes distinction — this study captures only what's
explicitly in the rules, not higher-order emergent strategy (p.187).

## 3. METHOD (pp.187–190)

**3.1 Source Text** (pp.187–188): only the D&D 5e *Player's Handbook* was
analyzed; DM's Guide, Monster Manual, and expansion books excluded (p.188)
— justified by most players only using the PHB, and by the scope being
already sizable for two researchers.

**3.2 Procedure for Ludemic Analysis** (pp.188–190): qualitative thematic
analysis, inductive (Thomas 2006; Merriam 2009), p.188; operational
definitions of "rule" and "ludeme" given (p.188); **four inclusion
criteria** (p.188): (1) a verb/process referring to a player, (2) a 3rd-
person can/cannot-do statement, (3) a 2nd-person direct instruction, (4) a
noun implying a player action (example given: "a passive check is a
special kind of ability check that doesn't involve any die rolls," 5e
*Player's Handbook* p.175); **exclusion criteria** — examples used to
illustrate an existing ludeme aren't separately coded (e.g., "swimming"
under "special movement") (p.188). Coding unit = sentence level, recorded
in Excel (p.189, also Figure 2 flowchart). Five-step procedure (pp.189–190):
(1) line-by-line coding → 379 ludemes identified (p.190); (2) constant
comparison/axial coding; (3) 37 formal categories defined; (4) 7
supercategories defined; (5) supercategories linked to learning factors.

## 4. RESULTS (pp.190–199)

**Table 1 — seven supercategories** (pp.191–192): 1. Performing an in-game
action (172 ludemes); 2. Role-playing (57); 3. Receiving information and
instructions (49); 4. Resolving actions and uncertainty (110); 5. Realizing
a character (144); 6. Table talking (33); 7. Serving as DM (5 — annotated
"Someone has to do it!").

**4.1 Performing an in-game action** (pp.193–194): goal setting — explicit
goals (completing a quest) vs. implicit goals (improving skills,
relationships), tied to self-regulated learning (Zimmerman 1989) and SCT
forethought (Bandura 2001), p.193; self-efficacy through mastery
experiences, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion (Bandura 1997; Schunk
1995), p.193; problem-solving and attention/recall (Anderson 1995), p.193;
observational learning, especially novice-from-expert in mixed groups
(Bandura 1971; Vygotsky 1978), p.193. Design principles (p.194):
scaffolded task complexity; varied NPC interactions requiring different
communication strategies (example: negotiating with a stubborn dwarf
leader vs. persuading a curious elf child).

**4.2 Role-playing** (p.194): intrinsic motivation via Self-Determination
Theory (Ryan & Deci 2000); Expectancy-Value Theory (Eccles et al. 1983);
embodied cognition (Wilson 2002); empathy/interpersonal skill development
(Goldstein & Winner 2012). Design principles (p.194): player agency over
character development; moral dilemmas without an obvious "right" answer.

**4.3 Receiving information and instructions** (pp.194–196): Information
Processing Theory (Miller 1956; Atkinson & Shiffrin 1968), p.194; Direct
Instruction (Rosenshine 1986) and its link to self-efficacy (Bandura 1986),
p.195; Zone of Proximal Development (Vygotsky 1978) and guided discovery
learning (Bruner 1961), p.195. Design principles (p.195): mix explicit and
implicit instruction; "learning by doing" instead of front-loading all
rules.

**4.4 Resolving actions and uncertainty** (pp.195–196): self-efficacy
(Bandura 1986; Schunk 1995), p.195; cognitive load theory (Paas & van
Merriënboer 1994; Sweller 1988), p.195; metacognition (Flavell 1979;
Brown 1987), pp.195–196. Design principles (p.196): embrace randomness/
uncertainty as a design choice; probabilistic elements to teach risk/
reward tradeoffs.

**4.5 Realizing a character** (pp.196–197): learner agency and
self-efficacy (Hmelo-Silver et al. 2007; Bandura 1997), p.196;
Expectancy-Value Theory applied to character creation (Wigfield & Eccles
2000), p.196; identity and belonging (Gee 2003; Subrahmanyam & Smahel
2011), p.196; self-actualization (Maslow 1968) and metacognition
(Zimmerman 2002), p.196. Design principles (p.197): character
customization with real gameplay impact; detailed progression systems
supporting diverse play styles.

**4.6 Table talking** (p.197): reciprocal determinism (Bandura 1986) and
collective efficacy (Bandura 2000); reflection and real-life transfer;
negotiation/perspective-taking/consensus-building as transferable life
skills. Design principles (p.197): foster group collaboration/negotiation;
design challenges requiring multiple characters' unique abilities.

**4.7 Serving as DM** (pp.197–199): only 5 ludemes identified because DM
rules mostly live in the (unanalyzed) *Dungeon Master's Guide* (pp.197–198);
DM role involves high metacognitive activity — real-time adaptation,
narrative design, rule interpretation (p.198); explicitly flagged as
outside this study's main scope, merits future dedicated study (p.198).
Design principles (pp.198–199): keep a GM/arbiter role for flexible,
improvisational play; provide substantial GM onboarding/support ("it's a
challenging job!").

## 5. CONCLUSIONS (pp.198–200)

- pp.198–199: taxonomy reframed as pivotal markers in the TTRPG learning
  trajectory; ludeme-level analysis enables hypothesis testing of whether
  learning occurred.
- p.199: **key limitation, verbatim**: "these connections remain simply
  hypothesized by the authors, as no empirical study was conducted with
  players in actual game settings" — explicitly linked to a broader
  problem in educational research generally lacking direct evidence (Cook
  et al. 2003; Wayne et al. 2008).
- p.199: justification for choosing D&D 5e — familiarity and
  representativeness, not claimed as the definitive TTRPG model.
- p.199: future work — expand to other TTRPGs; study the DM role in depth;
  study emergent higher-order strategies not specified in the rules text.
- p.200: closing call to move the field from anecdotal accounts of play
  toward evidence-based, systematic design.
