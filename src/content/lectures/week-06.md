There's more than one useful way to think about a clue, and this course
teaches two of them side by side rather than picking a winner. This
lecture introduces both before the studio applies them to your own draft.

Delivery format isn't just flavour — each one carries a different
practical risk. A literature clue, once a character starts looking, is
close to a guaranteed find, which makes it the right place for your
broadest, most load-bearing information rather than your best reveal. A
social clue that's hard-locked to a single NPC breaks the moment players
don't happen to talk to that NPC, so write it as something several NPCs
could plausibly say instead. And a trace clue — a footprint, a smell, a
scratch mark — is often the cheapest way to make a threat felt without
staging a full encounter with it.

Clues split into two broad categories. Guiding clues give the player
characters direction for where to explore and act next. Revealing clues
cover everything else — the solution to a puzzle, information about the
scenario's Mythos content — mainly used to push the plot forward and
explain it, while also working as a small reward for a player character
who actively investigates. As for how clues actually get planted, there
are three forms: literature-type clues, delivered through old books,
specific documents, diaries or notes; social-type clues, obtained through
talking with an NPC; and trace-type clues — physical evidence such as
fingerprints, footprints, claw marks, bullets, documents, burned
material, blood, saliva, hair, plant pollen, or a Deep One's scales.
Because pulling information straight out of a book is close to
guaranteed, the broadest and most load-bearing clues should be set up
this way; a social clue tied to one specific NPC can break the scenario
if the players happen to miss that NPC, so it's better to list out
everything obtainable through NPC conversation and hand it out flexibly
based on how the actual conversation goes.

## Outline

- function: does a clue guide players toward the next scene, or reveal
  what the truth actually means once they have it
- delivery: literature (documents, records), social (what an NPC says),
  or trace (physical evidence at a scene)
- a second lens: Blase's core clues (needed to progress), supporting
  clues (context and colour) and advantage clues (reward careful play
  without gating it)
- why redundancy — more than one path to a core clue — matters more than
  which taxonomy you use to think about it
- why the three delivery formats matter beyond flavour: literature clues
  are close to guaranteed once a character looks, social clues should
  never hard-lock to one NPC, and trace clues let a threat register
  without staging a direct encounter
