## 1. Hot Take

- Argues that a slow session focused on aftermath and cost isn't wasted time — it's essential.
- Quoted line: "The most useful slow session in a horror campaign is not filler."

## 2. What Campaign Pacing Actually Controls

- Pacing governs how fast investigators' knowledge, options, safety, and duties shift — not just event density.
- Four questions to ask after each session: what did the investigators learn; what became harder, more dangerous, or more expensive; what can they now choose that they could not before; what needs table time before the next escalation.
- States the fourth question is most often neglected, warning that skipping it turns "the campaign becomes a sequence of Keeper commands instead of an investigation."

## 3. Build a Campaign From Arcs, Not One Endless Plot

- An arc is a linked question-and-answer structure, not a single forced chain.
- Table of arc elements:

| Arc element | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Local case | Immediate problem | Body in locked conservatory |
| Recurring thread | Ties case to larger plot | Symbol matches earlier ledger |
| Human stake | Makes delay matter | Colleague blackmailed |
| Turning point | Reframes understanding | Cult is containing, not summoning |
| Arc decision | Costly choice | Expose truth vs. preserve lie |
| Aftermath | Shows change | Town safe, new enemy made |

- Advises running 2–3 active threads (urgent, personal, optional), not seven.
- Links to the Scenario Design Guide (source #5).

## 4. The Four Rhythms to Track

- **Information Rhythm**: broad leads early, method/goal mid-arc, cost/consequence late — answers should still feel like answers.
- **Threat Rhythm**: vary overt and implied threat (surveillance, deadlines, isolation, not just combat).
- **Emotional Rhythm**: ordinary stakes (relationships, money, guilt) make horror costly.
- **Mechanical Rhythm**: rotate activities (social, research, chase, fight, recovery); rolls should change situations. Links to the "How to Run Call of Cthulhu 7e" guide (source #10).

## 5. Plan a Scenario's Place in the Campaign

- Each scenario needs a one-sentence "campaign job."
- Table of scenario tempo:

| Position | Should do | Good ending feeling |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Establish competence/tone | Curiosity, clear next step |
| Deepening | Confirm recurring thread | Unease, wider horizon |
| Complication | Cost or divided loyalty | Doubt, urgency |
| Breather | Reflection, prep | Renewed purpose |
| Confrontation | Decisive threat | Relief or loss |
| Aftermath | New normal | Closure, optional hook |

## 6. Use Revelation Clocks, Not Constant Emergencies

- Clocks show opposition acting independently, not punishing delay.
- Worked example: an artifact broker moves shipment → silences witness → hosts sale → attempts ritual — each stage interceptable.
- Use clocks when opposition has independent plans, delay changes stakes, or players need to choose between leads.
- Keep private clocks in notes; reveal effects only in fiction, not as invisible deadlines.

## 7. Protect Investigation and Recovery Time

- Investigation needs "slack" — sessions shouldn't always open with an attack.
- Recovery scenes (medical care, research, family time) make consequences visible.
- Suggested surfacing techniques: an ally questioning continued work, distorted press coverage, family noticing change, bureaucratic complications, or an incompletely-contained clue.
- Links to the Sanity rules guide and the Major Wounds guide.

## 8. Vary the Shape of Danger

- Table of scenario shapes:

| Shape | Primary question | Paced with |
|---|---|---|
| Social pressure | Who can be trusted? | Bargains, alliances |
| Missing-person | Where/why did they go? | Reverse-life clues |
| Containment | How to stop spread? | Thresholds, priorities |
| Expedition | What's out there? | Travel, isolation |
| Infiltration | Learn truth unexposed? | Cover, observation |
| Disaster aftermath | Who's still at risk? | Rescue before explanation |

- Links to the Chase Rules guide.
- Key point: "Closer is often worse" than louder.

## 9. Escalate Consequences, Not Just Numbers

- Consequences should scale in kind, not just difficulty: early = private/reversible; mid = relational/resource loss; late = values-based dilemmas.
- Costs must match warnings given — unforeseeable costs feel "arbitrary" rather than tragic.
- Advises varying outcomes for allies (not all must die) to avoid teaching players not to bond.

## 10. Bring Back NPCs and Earlier Choices

- Recurring NPCs carry campaign memory.
- Before reintroducing one, ask: what changed for them; what do they want now; why are investigators relevant.
- Links to the NPC Creation Guide (source #11).
- States handouts should support, not solely carry, clues.

## 11. Know When an Arc Is Over

- An arc ends when its central question is answered and the required decision made — not when all threads are tied off.
- Pre-finale checklist: understand opposition's goal; consequences of inaction; available options and costs; relevant relationships.
- Advises following with an aftermath scene naming losses/gains before introducing new threats.

## 12. Campaign Pacing Worksheet

- A fill-in template covering: arc question, human stake, opposition goal, consequences of inaction, opening/mid-arc/finale beats, active threads, current rhythm beats, next clock event, recovery opportunity, and recurring NPC/choice callbacks.

## 13. Pacing Checklist

- A nine-point pre-session checklist including: current problem investigators can articulate; at least one resolved question; visible/discoverable pressure; opposition motivation independent of players; room for research/recovery; varied activity; proportional consequences; changed recurring elements; a scenario ending that resolves something.
