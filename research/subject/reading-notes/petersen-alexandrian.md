**Abstract / summary** (the post has no formal abstract — this is my
one-paragraph summary of the whole piece): Alexander distills a Petersen
seminar into a repeatable method for designing a CoC horror scenario from
almost nothing: start from three arbitrary "seed" elements (an image, a
place, an opponent), connect them into a "spine" that ties the villain's own
plan to the player characters, then develop supporting detail. Two rules
frame the whole thing — M.R. James's three principles for what makes a
ghost story land, and a "Creepy Stuff Rule" requiring three escalating
warnings before a monster can actually hurt a player character, so that harm
reads as earned rather than arbitrary. A worked example (an undead ancestor
named Heidvig, hot springs, and a burning-rabbit image) runs through most of
the piece to demonstrate the method end-to-end, followed by a grab-bag of
independent GMing tips.

## 1. Introduction (untitled)

Frames the piece as notes from a Petersen seminar video, reorganized by the
author into a step-by-step model.

## 2. THE BASICS ACCORDING TO M.R. JAMES

Three rules Petersen borrows from ghost-story writer M.R. James:

1. **The ghost is malign** — the antagonist can't secretly have good
   intentions; the threat has to stay genuinely hostile.
2. **A setting the players can imagine themselves in** — Petersen argues
   Lovecraft was writing "cutting edge thrillers," not historical fiction.
   *Example*: he speculates that if Lovecraft had written in 1945 rather
   than the 1920s, a character might use a nuke against Cthulhu instead of a
   steamship — the point being immediacy matters more than the specific era;
   other settings work, but need extra effort to feel real and present.
3. **No jargon** — originally about avoiding theosophist pseudo-science
   terms; Petersen reinterprets it as favoring fiction-first description over
   mechanics-first language, since horror doesn't come from game stats.

## 3. THE SCENARIO SEED

Three raw, fairly arbitrary starting ingredients:

- **A scene/image** — *Example*: burning a stuffed rabbit, from the picture
  book *The Velveteen Rabbit*; a strong emotional image.
- **A place** — *Example*: hot springs.
- **An opponent** — *Example*: a 500-year-old undead ancestor.

## 4. THE SCENARIO SPINE

Steps that turn the seed into a structure, demonstrated on the same
running example:

- **A. Find connections** between seed elements — *Example*: tie the undead
  ancestor to the hot springs (he lives in/is bound to them).
- **B. Connect PCs to the villain** — *Example*: one PC is a descendant of
  the ancestor, named **Heidvig**, making the horror personal and giving a
  built-in hook.
- **C. Start from the villain's plan** — *Example*: Heidvig calls a "family
  reunion," whose real purpose is transferring his consciousness into a
  younger relative.
- **D. Make sure every seed element gets used** — *Example*: the "burning
  rabbit" image is explained by having Heidvig hand out scrimshaw (carved
  bone) reliquaries containing fragments of his old body, letting him track
  his descendants through them.

## 5. DEVELOP DETAILS

Once the spine exists, fill in specifics:

- *Example*: cultist staff/servants who might accidentally slip and refer to
  Heidvig by his cult title, "The One Who's Below."
- *Example*: tying Heidvig to Innsmouth's Captain Obed Marsh, and modeling
  the consciousness-transfer ritual on Lovecraft's own story "The Thing on
  the Doorstep" — keeping consistency with existing Mythos fiction where
  convenient.

## 6. THE CREEPY STUFF RULE

Players must get three escalating warnings before a monster can strike, so
harm feels self-inflicted rather than arbitrary. Demonstrated on the same
Heidvig/hot-springs scenario:

1. **Hint** — smell of cooked flesh, evidence of boiled skin.
2. **Clear indication of danger** — discovering a deeper hot-spring level
   next to a lava pit, plus supporting evidence/bodies.
3. **The monster** — Heidvig himself appears.

Elsewhere in the article (Random Tips section, but logically part of this
rule): *"you can't just open a random door and then be eaten by the
shoggoth"* — a one-line generic anti-example for skipping the escalation.

## 7. THE SOLUTION

The GM prepares at least one workable way to resolve/escape the threat
(while staying open to player-invented alternatives).

- *Example*: destroying the scrimshaw reliquary in the lava severs Heidvig's
  link to his target descendant. This also closes the loop on the seed's
  "burning rabbit" image — Heidvig's old body must burn away in the lava for
  the transfer ritual to complete, so what looks like "stopping the villain"
  is really preventing him from finishing his own self-destructive ritual.
  (The article's treatment of this twist is brief — it's a demonstration of
  "closing the loop on the seed," not a fully worked-out plot reveal.)

## 8. RANDOM TIPS & INSIGHTS

Independent tips, not tied to the Heidvig example:

- **Summarize NPC-to-NPC dialogue** rather than voicing both sides of an
  NPC conversation yourself.
- **The "peripheral vision" trick** — borrowed from the film *Night of the
  Demon*: a character catches something in the corner of their eye, or
  someone else asks about an object/animal that isn't actually there.
- **The "moving object" scare** — from M.R. James's own story "The Treasure
  of Abbot Thomas": a seemingly inanimate object (a satchel) turns out to be
  alive.
- **Allow failure** — treat failure as a source of interesting complication,
  not something to prevent.
- **Give NPCs a way to disengage** — an urgent task, a pager going off,
  suddenly not understanding the PCs — because players tend to interrogate
  relentlessly, "like a hammerhead shark."
- **Plan for police involvement in advance** — *Example*: cultist cops, so
  that calling the police is a dead end or actively backfires rather than
  something the GM has to improvise on the spot.
