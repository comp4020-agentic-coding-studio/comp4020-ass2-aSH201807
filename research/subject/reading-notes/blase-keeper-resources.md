## 2. Introductory framing

- Opens by reframing the Keeper's job: not "protecting your plot from player choices," but preparing mysteries so choices carry consequences.
- Describes the Keeper's workload as: truth, clues, threats, NPCs, rules, tone, pacing.
- States the prep goal is not exhaustive detail but readiness to answer four questions: what happened; what changes if no one intervenes; what evidence can reveal it; which people will make the investigators care.
- Embedded newsletter signup box ("Get the Free GM Session Kit" — a Session Zero template, recap format, pricing cheat sheet) — noted as site monetization, not article content.

## 3. Hot Take

- States the Keeper's role is making "success costly, intelligible, and worth pursuing" — explicitly not engineering failure.

## 4. What the Keeper Is Responsible For

- Five things to know before a session: the truth, the active threat, the evidence, the people, the likely costs.

## 5. Start With a Table Agreement

- Recommends discussing, before play: tone, excluded/faded topics, sensitive historical material, PvP conflict, safety tools, investigator group dynamics.

## 6. Prepare the Truth, Not a Script

- Advises writing a causal-chain "spine" (antagonist goal → past actions → next steps → consequences) instead of a fixed scene order.
- Worked example: a collector uses a forged diary to locate a hidden object in an abandoned estate, hires criminals to keep people away, and races a storm to perform a rite that summons something inhuman.
- Quoted line: "That is enough to generate locations, witnesses, evidence, and escalation."
- For each location, the Keeper should note: what's visible, what can be discovered, and what changes if the players linger or make noise.

## 7. Build an Investigation That Moves

- Recommends a "clue map built from questions rather than scenes," with multiple evidence paths per conclusion so no single die roll gates the whole mystery.
- The article's table maps four investigative questions to evidence types and outcomes:
  - Identifying the culprit → witnesses/records/physical clues/expert input → a suspect.
  - Understanding the current threat → (same evidence-type pool) → a location.
  - Learning its timeline → (same evidence-type pool) → a deadline.
  - Finding a way to stop it → (same evidence-type pool) → an intervention choice.
  - (Note: the exact per-row evidence-type mapping wasn't preserved distinctly across rows in what I could retrieve — the four evidence types and four outcomes are confirmed, but I can't confirm which evidence type maps to which specific question/outcome without over-claiming; flagged rather than guessed.)
- Quoted line: failure should "complicate the investigation, not erase its core evidence."

## 8. Deliver Information Fairly

- Basic/obvious facts should be given freely, without a roll.
- Rolls should govern "speed, depth, safety, or advantage" rather than gate essential clues outright.
- On handouts: they work best when they "create a question the group can discuss."

## 9. Create NPCs Who Produce Choices

- NPCs are built from four elements: Goal, Fear, Resource, Limit.
- Examples given: a goal of suppressing a scandal; a fear of a family member's cult ties; a resource such as a key or testimony; a limit such as refusing to betray a friend without proof.
- Explicit design principle: vary NPC trustworthiness so "reliable people exist" — this is what makes betrayal meaningful when it happens.

## 10. Use Backstory Hooks With Permission

- Encourages using investigator backstories as consent-based hooks.
- Warns against overusing one player's backstory, and against making backstory hooks purely punitive.

## 11. Pace Discovery, Danger, and Rest

- Five-beat session rhythm: (1) open with a troubling development that demands response; (2) let the group investigate; (3) shift the pressure (deadline, threat, cost of delay); (4) allow a decision based on available information; (5) deliver an aftermath before escalating again.

## 12. Run Action as a Change of Pressure

- Frames action scenes as serving the investigation, not replacing it.
- Links to two sub-guides: Combat and Chases Guide (`/articles/coc-7e/combat-chases`) — "player-facing survival framework"; Major Wounds Guide (`/articles/coc-7e/combat-chases/major-wounds`) — injury consequences.

## 15. Separate Tablecraft From 7e Procedures

- A table contrasts general GMing advice against specific 7e mechanics, across five Keeper needs:

  - Stuck players → Idea rolls.

## 17. Keeper Session Checklist

- **Before the session**: confirm boundaries/tone; write truth/threat/stakes; prepare multiple evidence paths; build NPCs with goal/fear/resource/limit; identify permitted backstory hooks; decide which scenes need 7e procedures.
- **During the session**: state the obvious before rolling; ask player goals not just skills; preserve core evidence while letting failure add cost; share spotlight; end scenes on resolution; keep danger visible.
- **After the session**: record what changed; update antagonist plans; track NPC relationship shifts; prepare next session's opening around a consequence.
